Purpose

DBA's design process includes initial site analysis as one input into our preliminary concept design process, along with client goals, a site visit, and a first test fit of program and massing. A clear response to the site early in a project informs future decision-making. Site analysis supports our core design principles, informing how “design for human experience,” “connect to place,” and “bet on the future” become specific decisions on an actual project rather than statements of intent.

Protocol

Historically, our site analysis process has varied from project to project, depending on staffing and time resourced. We have worked to support and standardize the process by developing curated resources in the Resource Center, and are building out our protocol to support more consistent execution and incorporate automation where appropriate — Sitekick, and this dashboard, are part of that effort.

Why a dashboard

A Sitekick report is the record at a point in time. This dashboard is the same research made workable in the field: every value stays clickable to its source, every hand correction is logged rather than silently overwriting the machine-pulled figure, and two team members editing the same brief see each other's changes rather than clobbering them.

Organization

We document the basic site identity first — coordinates, parcel, size, current use, adjacent context — then research falls into Sitekick's ten sections, with curiosity and openness to what the site turns up beyond the checklist. That research is assessed for assets, gaps, hazards, and opportunities relevant to the site and the larger community.

Site intro

01

What this place is

10 Kirk Avenue is a 4.18-acre parcel (APN 599-39-112) in the Alum Rock / East Foothills area of East San Jose, at the base of the Diablo Range. County records classify it as a convalescent hospital / skilled-nursing use; it operates as the California PEO Home, San Jose Unit — an existing residential-care campus rather than a vacant lot. There is no DBA project number or defined program yet; this brief reads the site as found, and the population it already serves — older adults in a care setting — is the lens most likely to matter for whatever the site becomes.

The site sits above the valley floor near Alum Rock Park and Penitencia Creek, at the transition from the residential East Side flatlands to the oak-woodland foothills. Surrounding fabric is low-rise single-family residential and institutional; the neighborhood is a long-established, family-oriented Latino and Vietnamese community centered on schools such as nearby Linda Vista Elementary. Regional access is by car — the foothill location is well east of the Berryessa BART station and the light-rail and bus corridors that anchor the flatland Eastside.

Defining site characteristics

  1. Large by East San Jose standards — 4.18 acres in single ownership, an unusual amount of contiguous developable land in an established neighborhood.
  2. Currently occupied by an active skilled-nursing / residential-care use, so redevelopment or expansion carries an existing vulnerable population and an operating entitlement to reckon with.
  3. Foothill-edge position at the base of the Diablo Range — the wildland-urban interface, slope, and oak-woodland setting all begin here.
  4. Within a few kilometers of the Calaveras Fault; the 2007 M5.6 Alum Rock earthquake originated in Alum Rock Park just north of the site.
  5. Low measured environmental burden (CalEnviroScreen tract 6085504201 ranks near the 2nd percentile for pollution burden) — the severe burden of the flatland Eastside is west of here, not on the site.
  6. Car-dependent location with a Walk Score of 0 for the 95127 area — day-to-day errands assume a vehicle, which matters for a non-driving resident population.

Most likely hazards, in order. Strong seismic ground shaking from the nearby Calaveras and Hayward faults is the dominant condition; then chronic climate stressors — increasing extreme-heat days and near-annual wildfire smoke; then wildfire and PSPS de-energization exposure from the adjacent wildland edge; with surface flooding low (FEMA Zone D) but unconfirmed at the parcel. The rating table is in Section 10.

Finding highlights

  • The site already houses a population that cannot easily evacuate — every hazard here is really a shelter-in-place question.
  • Seismic shaking, not flood, is the defining physical constraint: the Calaveras Fault runs through these foothills.
  • A rare 4-acre parcel with low pollution burden and a foothill setting — the asset is the land and the outlook, the constraints are access and the wildland edge.

Design considerations

02

Ten findings that rise above technical compliance to a design conversation, highest-stakes first. At least one addresses community and cultural context. Findings and opportunities the design team weighs — not directives.

1.A care population makes resilience a life-safety program, not an amenity

Finding. The site serves older adults in a skilled-nursing setting — residents who may not be able to leave quickly in a heat wave, smoke event, power loss, or earthquake. The regional hazards here (shaking, heat, smoke, de-energization) all land hardest on exactly this population.

Design opportunity or constraint. Whether the program stays as care or changes, the ability to shelter in place with power, cooling, and clean air becomes a core requirement rather than an add-on. A single conditioned, backup-powered common space can serve as both a cooling and a clean-air refuge — a question of where it goes and how it is powered.

2.Seismic performance is set by the fault, not just the code minimum

Finding. The Calaveras Fault runs through the East San Jose foothills within a few kilometers of the site, and the Hayward Fault lies a short distance west; the 2007 M5.6 Alum Rock earthquake nucleated in Alum Rock Park just to the north. Design ground motions here are high.

Design opportunity or constraint. For an occupancy that shelters in place, the conversation is whether to target performance above life-safety minimums — immediate-occupancy or functional-recovery objectives — so the building and its power and water stay usable after a nearby event. A structural engineer sets the design category and ground-motion parameters.

3.The wildland edge is an asset and an exposure at the same time

Finding. The parcel sits at the foothill / wildland-urban interface below the Diablo Range, next to Alum Rock Park. That gives it oak-woodland outlook and open space, and also puts it near CAL FIRE fire-hazard territory and the PSPS de-energization footprint that comes with foothill utility lines.

Design opportunity or constraint. The design question is how to hold the view and the connection to the park while defending the building — defensible-space planting, ember-resistant assemblies and screened vents at the uphill edge, weighed against openness. Confirm the parcel's fire-hazard-severity-zone status before assuming it drives the envelope.

4.A rare large parcel in an established neighborhood

Finding. At 4.18 acres in single ownership, this is an uncommon amount of contiguous land in built-out East San Jose. Whatever the future program, the site can hold a real mix — care, housing, open space, community use — rather than a single building maximized on a small lot.

Design opportunity or constraint. The opportunity is a phased, mixed program that keeps the existing care use running while adding to it. Site area also makes on-grade parking, generous landscape, and resilient infrastructure feasible — the constraint is that generosity of land can invite low density where the neighborhood need may argue for more homes.

5.Car-dependence is a real barrier for a non-driving population

Finding. The 95127 area scores 0 for walkability — errands assume a car, and the foothill location is far from the light-rail and BART that serve the flatland Eastside. For residents who don't drive, that isolates them from groceries, clinics, and gathering places.

Design opportunity or constraint. The design response is whether to bring some of those services onto the site — ground-floor clinic, cafe, or shared community room — and to make the shuttle / drop-off / accessible-transit arrival a designed front door rather than a back-of-house condition.

6.Design with the Eastside's Latino and Vietnamese community, not just for it

Finding. Alum Rock is a long-established, family-oriented Latino and Vietnamese neighborhood — historically a Chicano and Mexican-American district, with a large Vietnamese community anchored in the broader Eastside. It has also carried East San Jose's history of disinvestment.

Design opportunity or constraint. Multigenerational and multilingual use, culturally legible gathering and food space, and early engagement with neighborhood and community-development partners are the conversation — how the project reads as belonging to this community rather than arriving in it.

7.Heat is the climate stressor that changes the most over the building's life

Finding. San Jose's Mediterranean climate is mild today, but downscaled projections point to a rising count of extreme-heat days. For an aging population, heat is a direct health risk, and the current building stock in this area was not built for it.

Design opportunity or constraint. Passive strategies — orientation, external shading, cross-ventilation, thermal mass, a tight envelope, and shaded outdoor rooms — are on the table now, sized to mid- and late-century heat rather than today's normals. Pull the parcel's Cal-Adapt heat projection to set the target.

8.A clean grid moves the carbon fight to embodied carbon

Finding. San Jose is served by Silicon Valley Clean Energy, whose default product is essentially carbon-free, and the city requires all-electric new construction. Operational emissions from a well-designed all-electric building here are very low.

Design opportunity or constraint. With operations largely decarbonized, the largest available reductions are in materials — a low-embodied-carbon structure and envelope. Roof area then trades between PV (for resilience and backup) and other uses; that allocation is a design decision, not a given.

9.Foothill ground is a geotechnical question before it is a design one

Finding. The site sits at the transition from valley alluvium to foothill bedrock. Liquefaction risk is generally lower here than on the fill and young alluvium of the valley floor to the west, but slope, older alluvial fan deposits, and possible fill from prior grading all vary across a 4-acre parcel.

Design opportunity or constraint. Foundation type, cut-and-fill balance, and where taller structure can sit all wait on a project geotechnical investigation. Early borings would let massing follow the good ground rather than fighting it.

10.Water is a dry-season storage question, not a flood one

Finding. Rain here is winter-concentrated (roughly 80% falls November–March) with essentially dry summers, and the region carries a standing drought signal. The site drains toward the Coyote Creek watershed via the Penitencia Creek system; surface-flood risk on the parcel appears low.

Design opportunity or constraint. The trade-off is whether to store winter runoff for dry-season landscape irrigation versus treat-and-release under the city's C.3 stormwater rules — a sizing and cost question. Drought-adapted, largely native planting reduces the demand either way.

Land

03

The parcel lies at the western foot of the Diablo Range, where the Santa Clara Valley alluvial plain meets the foothills. Regional mapping places it near the transition from Quaternary alluvial-fan and stream deposits (shed from the Penitencia/Coyote drainages) to older, firmer foothill materials and bedrock upslope. This transitional position generally means lower liquefaction susceptibility than the young alluvium and artificial fill of the valley floor to the west — but slope, variable fan thickness, and any undocumented fill from the existing campus's original grading make that a parcel-specific question.

For design Foundation type, the cut-and-fill strategy on sloping ground, and where the heaviest structure can sit all depend on conditions that only borings resolve. On a large parcel, an early geotechnical investigation lets massing follow competent ground rather than driving deep foundations across the whole site.

History + culture

04

Before European settlement this was Tamien Ohlone land, within the territory the Muwekma Ohlone Tribe traces today; the foothills and Penitencia Creek were oak-woodland and riparian country used for acorn, game, and water. The valley below was Spanish and Mexican rancho land, then some of the most productive orchard country in California — East San Jose's flats were subdivided from fruit ranches into homes through the 1940s and 50s. Alum Rock became a heavily Chicano and Mexican-American district and, from the 1980s–90s, home to a large Vietnamese community; it has also carried East San Jose's long history of public disinvestment relative to the west side of the city.

For design The people this part of the city was built by and for — working Latino and Vietnamese families, and an Indigenous presence that predates all of it — are the people the project should be designed with. That argues for early, multilingual engagement, culturally legible gathering and food space, and a land acknowledgment developed in conversation with the Muwekma Ohlone rather than asserted.

Climate + weather

06

San Jose is California Building Climate Zone 4, a warm-summer Mediterranean climate: dry, mild, and sunny. Summer highs average around 82 °F in July–August with cool nights; winter lows sit in the low 40s °F. Annual rainfall at the valley station is about 15.8 in, roughly 80% of it falling November–March with essentially dry summers — the foothill site likely runs somewhat wetter than the airport gauge. Prevailing summer wind is a modest afternoon NW breeze drawn through the valley; the foothills give some overnight drainage flow.

Background air quality is generally moderate, with the main degradations episodic — summer ozone and, increasingly, wildfire smoke that can push the region into unhealthy ranges for days at a time. The foothill-residential setting is relatively quiet; the nearest steady noise sources are neighborhood arterials rather than a freeway or rail line at the property.

For design The climate rewards passive design — good solar orientation, generous external shading on south and west, cross-ventilation to use cool nights, and shaded outdoor rooms. Because the real air threat is episodic smoke rather than chronic pollution, a sealable, filtered clean-air mode for at least one refuge space matters more here than continuous heavy filtration everywhere.

Ecology / habitat

07

The site sits in EPA's Central California Foothills and Coastal Mountains ecoregion, at the western foot of the Diablo Range. Pre-development cover here was oak woodland and savanna — coast live oak, valley oak, and blue oak over annual grassland — grading into the Penitencia Creek riparian corridor and the orchard-turned-residential valley below.

The developed campus offers limited existing habitat, but the foothill edge and adjacent park make this a meaningful wildlife-connectivity location — nesting raptors and migratory birds use the oak-grassland mosaic, and creek corridors nearby support riparian species. An official listed-species list should be pulled at the coordinate; the foothill / creek context raises the usual candidates (nesting birds under the MBTA, and potentially special-status amphibians or serpentine-associated species in the wider area).

For design On a 4-acre foothill parcel there is real habitat opportunity at the ground plane — oak-woodland restoration, native bunchgrass, and pollinator planting that reconnect the site to Alum Rock Park. Because the site is on a bird-movement edge, bird-safe glazing and lighting should enter the conversation early rather than as a late correction, and any tree removal be checked against nesting season.

City goals + funding context San José's Community Forest Management Plan (2022) sets a goal of 20% tree canopy by 2051, reversing a decline from 15.4% (2012) to ~13.2% (2020), and names canopy growth in disadvantaged communities as a priority. The city won a $6.6M USDA Forest Service urban-forestry grant (2023, Inflation Reduction Act) for underserved neighborhoods that explicitly funds vegetation management at Alum Rock Park and Overfelt Gardens — both adjacent to this site. A generous planting scheme here aligns with that goal and could draw on CAL FIRE Urban & Community Forestry grants (state GGRF + IRA, disadvantaged-community priority) and a planting/stewardship partnership with Our City Forest. Alignment and funding pursuit are the design team's and client's call, not a commitment — confirm current grant cycles and amounts.

Water systems

08

The parcel drains toward the Coyote Creek watershed by way of the Upper Penitencia Creek system, which comes out of Alum Rock Park just north of the site. As an existing developed campus it is largely impervious today; redevelopment is an opportunity to add pervious area and treatment. Groundwater sits in the Santa Clara Subbasin managed by Valley Water; at the foothill edge the water table is generally deeper than on the valley floor, and sea-level-rise-driven groundwater emergence — a real issue near the Bay margin — does not reach this inland, elevated site.

Potable supply and sewer are municipal (San José / Valley Water). The live design question is dry-season resilience, not flooding: with rain concentrated in a few winter months and long dry summers, on-site capture and reuse (rainwater for irrigation, potentially greywater) trades against the cost and footprint of storage versus simply meeting the city's C.3 treat-and-release stormwater requirements. Which way that goes is a sizing and program decision, not a foregone system.

For design Flooding is not the driver here; drought resilience is. The near-term moves are a low-water, largely native landscape and a stormwater approach that at minimum meets C.3, with rainwater storage evaluated against dry-season irrigation demand. Groundwater depth and infiltration rates for any bioretention need a geotechnical percolation test.

Energy

09

Electricity is delivered on PG&E wires but supplied by Silicon Valley Clean Energy, San José's community-choice aggregator, whose default GreenStart product is essentially carbon-free. The regional grid (EPA eGRID CAMX subregion) is already relatively low-carbon, and with SVCE's default product the operational emissions of a well-designed all-electric building here are very low.

San José requires all-electric new construction, so the site is electrification-ready by policy — confirm the current ordinance and any exceptions. Solar potential is good in this sunny, low-fog inland location and should be quantified by running PVWatts at the coordinate. Because operations are largely decarbonized, embodied carbon in the structure and envelope becomes the dominant lifecycle contribution — the place where material choices move the number most.

For design The largest available carbon reductions are in materials, not operations — a low-embodied-carbon structure earns more than squeezing an already-clean energy load. Rooftop PV still earns its place, but here primarily for resilience: paired with storage it keeps a care population's refuge space cooled, filtered, and powered through a PSPS event or post-earthquake outage. Roof area then trades between PV and other uses.
