The read

The Petaluma Fairgrounds sits flat on Petaluma River floodplain alluvium, a century-old civic event campus — grandstand, exhibit halls, racetrack oval, and expansive parking — that has hosted the county fair continuously since 1936. Flooding is the best-documented and most likely hazard here, followed by strong ground shaking from the nearby Hayward-Rodgers Creek fault system, then near-annual wildfire smoke and PG&E power shutoffs each fall — regional pressures layered onto a site whose entitlement path is itself temporary, pending the city’s own master plan and permanent zoning for the property.

Flood and liquefaction risk here read as one story, not two: both are floodplain-wide conditions shared with much of low-lying Petaluma, not a parcel anomaly, which argues for coordinating any site-level flood or foundation strategy with the city’s broader floodplain planning rather than treating the fairgrounds as an isolated problem. The same logic holds for identity — the site’s agricultural-heritage story sits next to a history the city itself only acknowledged in 2025, when it renamed downtown’s Center Park to Historic Chinatown Park in recognition of the exclusion of its Chinese immigrant community from that same agricultural economy.

Key early insight. Confirm the FEMA flood zone and base flood elevation before schematic design — it is the single highest-leverage unknown on this site, and it will drive first-floor elevation and ground-floor use.

Design considerations

Flooding is the dominant physical constraint, and the exact zone isn’t confirmed yet.
The parcel sits on Petaluma River floodplain alluvium in a city that has flooded five times since 1982; it is very likely within a FEMA-mapped Special Flood Hazard Area, but the exact zone, base flood elevation, and whether the SFHA boundary crosses the parcel need a direct FEMA Map Service Center pull — the team’s question is how first-floor elevation, ground-floor use, and grading strategy should respond once that’s known.
A temporary overlay zone makes the entitlement path itself a moving target.
The site is governed today by a Fairgrounds Overlay Zone explicitly described as a placeholder while the city prepares a master plan and permanent zoning for the property. Any project here is designing into a regulatory framework that may shift during the project’s own timeline, raising the question of how early and continuous coordination with Planning and the Sonoma-Marin Agricultural Association should shape the schedule.
This is Coast Miwok land, and the fairgrounds’ agricultural identity carries an exclusionary history alongside its celebrated one.
The site lies within Coast Miwok territory, possibly near a Southern Pomo boundary, with the Federated Indians of Graton Rancheria as the federally recognized tribe today; Petaluma’s “Egg Basket of the World” identity also grew alongside a documented 1877–1900s campaign that violently excluded the city’s Chinese immigrant community, acknowledged by the city only in 2025. A master plan leaning on agricultural heritage has an opening to ask whose story that heritage tells, and how tribal engagement goes directly to FIGR rather than generic acknowledgment language.
